Project 01 | Version 01

V1 - Initial Build

STRYKER V1

First version of STRYKER. Built to prove I could design, assemble, and manually control a full 6 DOF arm from scratch.

DOF

6 (yaw, shoulder, elbow, wrist x2, gripper)

Fabrication

FDM 3D printed, PLA

Control

ESP32 + dedicated servo driver

Power

6V, bulk capacitors for transient suppression

Goal

Manual control and motion validation

Outcome

Full 6-joint manual operation

Mechanical

  • Designed the arm around three simple goals: enough reach, smooth joint motion, and a structure I could actually build
  • Printed the structure in PLA and kept refining fit, alignment, and joint clearances until all six joints moved cleanly
  • Ended with full joint-by-joint manual control, which proved the arm could work as a user-driven pick-and-place platform

Electronics & Actuation

  • Used an ESP32, a servo driver, and breadboard wiring so each joint could be tested one at a time
  • The 6V power system included bulk capacitors and a power switch to keep testing stable when the servos pulled sudden current

Lessons Carried into V2

V1 proved the arm could work, but it also exposed the next problems to solve: bulk, wiring, clearances, and accuracy. Those issues directly shaped the shorter, cleaner autonomous redesign in V2.

Project 01 | Version 02

V2 - Autonomous Pick and Place

STRYKER V2

Second version of STRYKER. I rebuilt the arm to be smaller, cleaner, and capable of autonomous pick and place.

DOF

6 (J1 yaw, J2-J3 pitch, J4-J5 wrist, J6 gripper)

Controller

Arduino Nano + PCA9685 PWM driver

Vision

Overhead iPhone + workspace markers

Software

Python / OpenCV pipeline

Calibration

Camera mapping + measured servo calibration

Outcome

Completed three consecutive autonomous pick-and-place cycles

Redesign

  • Shortened the links, cleaned up the layout, and made the arm less bulky than V1 so it could move more smoothly
  • Checked reach, balance, and tipping risk before fabrication so the new version would stay stable while still covering the workspace

Computer Vision Pipeline

  • Before the arm could pick up the block, it first had to know where the block was on the table. I mounted an iPhone above the workspace so the whole area stayed visible in one simple top-down camera view
  • I placed four markers around the workspace so the software could learn how positions in the camera image matched real positions on the table. That way, the robot was not just seeing pixels. It was understanding where the block was in real space
  • From there, I used OpenCV to look specifically for the red block and ignore most of the rest of the scene. Once the block was found, that location became the target for the arm

Electronics

  • Designed a custom carrier board that combined the Arduino Nano and PCA9685 and routed all six servo signal lines cleanly
  • Moving off the breadboard removed wiring issues and made the arm much easier to assemble, test, and debug

Inverse Kinematics & Servo Calibration

  • Once the robot knew where the block was, the next problem was figuring out how each joint should move to reach it. I used inverse kinematics, which is the math that turns one target point into angle commands for the base, shoulder, elbow, and wrist
  • That worked well as a starting point, but real servos do not move as perfectly as the math assumes. Even when the target location was correct, the real arm could still land a little off
  • To make it more accurate, I tested real arm positions and used those measurements to calibrate the servo commands. That helped the software control the physical arm more reliably, not just the ideal version of it on paper

Autonomous Pick Sequence

  • After the block was detected and the arm knew how to reach it, the robot followed the same routine every time: move above the block, lower down, close the gripper, lift the block, carry it to the drop zone, release it, and move back up
  • I also smoothed the servo motion so the arm would not jump sharply between positions. That made the movement steadier, reduced shaking, and helped the robot complete three consecutive autonomous pick-and-place cycles

Challenges & Next Steps

  • The hardest part was not making the robot work once. It was making it work the same way over and over again. Small camera errors or small servo errors could build up and cause misses after a few runs
  • The next step would be tighter calibration and live visual feedback, so the arm could keep checking where the block is while it moves and correct itself in real time instead of relying only on the first measurement

Project 02 - Controls and Mechatronics

SENTINEL

A self-balancing ball robot built from scratch. The goal was to keep an unstable robot standing upright by combining drivetrain design, sensors, and real-time control.

Platform

Basketball + omni wheels

Power

12V battery, 2A motor drivers

Sensing

IMU (gyro + accel), encoders

Control

Balance-first PID control

Comms

Bluetooth controller interface

Outcome

Self-balanced for 64 seconds

Motor Sizing

  • A ball robot is always trying to fall over, so the motors have to react fast enough to roll the ball underneath the robot and catch it
  • I estimated the torque needed for that correction, then chose the motors and gearbox around that load so the robot would have enough power to balance

Motion Model

  • I also had to understand how motor rotation would actually move the ball under the robot
  • That motion model connected a small tilt error to the wheel response needed to correct it, which is what made closed-loop balancing possible

Sensors, Control & Logging

  • The IMU measured tilt and angular motion, and the encoders tracked how the wheels were turning. Together, they gave the controller the feedback it needed to decide how to respond
  • Bluetooth let me send commands and re-zero the robot during testing, and UART logging let me tune the system from real data instead of guessing

Balance Controller

  • I built separate control loops for balancing and steering, but balancing was the main challenge and the first milestone I focused on fully completing
  • I tuned the P, I, and D gains through repeated live tests and filtered the IMU data so sensor noise would not make the controller overreact
  • With that control loop in place, the robot could keep making small corrections fast enough to stay upright

Result

SENTINEL successfully balanced for 64 seconds on top of the ball. Steering was the next step, but balancing for over a minute was the clearest proof that the drivetrain, sensors, and control logic were all working together.

Project 05 - Embedded Systems

RANGER

A low-cost autonomous robot that tracked its own position, avoided obstacles, found a bright orange target, and stored detections in a simple 2D map.

Main Controller

BeagleBone Blue

Sensor MCU

Arduino Nano

Drive

Differential, DC motors w/ encoders

Sensors

Ultrasonic sonar, USB camera, thermopile

Perception

HSV blob tracking + thermal thresholding

Outcome

Obstacle avoidance, orange target approach, 2D mapping

Odometry & Error Analysis

  • Used encoder counts and IMU heading to estimate where the robot was as it moved
  • Ran repeated square-path trials and compared the final pose against ruler measurements to measure drift and show where feedback control was needed

Closed-Loop Control

  • Used simple waypoint math. The robot first turned toward the next point, then drove the straight-line distance to get there
  • Added heading and distance feedback so it could correct drift while moving instead of trusting dead reckoning alone

Navigation & Obstacle Avoidance

  • Used right, front, and left ultrasonic sonars to watch clearance while driving
  • Set simple distance thresholds for stop and clear states, then used obstacle-avoidance routines to move around the object and return to the path

Thermal Sensing & Signal Conditioning

  • Built the thermal sensing stage around an analog front end, averaged the ADC signal to steady it, and used a 1.2 V threshold to classify hot versus not hot

Vision & Mapping

  • Used a USB camera and color filtering to detect a bright orange target, then tracked blob size and image location to decide whether it was visible, centered, or close
  • Used a blob size threshold of 15 pixels and re-centering steps before moving forward, then stored detections in a simple 2D grid map

Project 04 - Controls and Embedded

LEONARDO

A 2-axis drawing robot that turned input images into motor commands and reproduced them physically on paper for under $200.

Controller

Arduino Nano 33 IoT w/ Motor Carrier

Actuation

2 DC motors w/ encoders + 1 servo

Build Cost

~$200

Output

Mona Lisa, Michigan M, US map, and custom images

Best Runtime

Michigan M in about 8 minutes

Torque Analysis & Workspace Mapping

  • Mapped the workspace in MATLAB and cut out regions the arm could not reach cleanly
  • Used torque limits to keep drawings inside areas where the motors stayed in a safe working range, below 35 percent of stall torque

Software Architecture

  • Converted image pixels into line segments, then turned those segments into joint commands through inverse kinematics and encoder feedback
  • Replaced the original left-to-right scan order with a nearest-segment search, which saved roughly 0.5 to 1 second per segment

Result

LEONARDO was fully built and demonstrated on paper. It drew the Mona Lisa, the Michigan M, a U.S. map, and other custom inputs. The biggest gain came from the path-planning update, which cut the Michigan M from about 10 minutes to about 8 minutes at the same quality.

Project 06 - Embedded Systems

PATHFINDER

A one-week line-following robot built to prove that cheap sensors and a simple control stack could still produce reliable autonomous motion.

Platform

MBot (differential drive)

Microcontroller

Raspberry Pi Pico (MicroPython)

Line Sensing

7-ch IR reflectance array (I2C)

Feedback

Quadrature encoders, 48:1 gear ratio

Control

Bang-bang to PID cascade

Smoothing

Exponential filter (alpha about 0.75)

Timeline

Built in 1 week

Outcome

Reliable full-course completion

Encoder & Motor Control

  • Used interrupt-driven quadrature decoding in MicroPython so wheel motion was captured without polling delays, then drove the motors with 10 kHz PWM and verified wheel speed on the oscilloscope

PID Wheel Speed Controller

  • Built a discrete PID speed controller, tuned it from logged speed and current data, and added saturation handling to prevent windup

Line Sensor (I2C / PCA9557)

  • Used a 7-channel reflectance array with a PCA9557 I/O expander to read the line through I2C
  • The hardest part was setting the sensor height low enough to see the line clearly but high enough not to scrape the floor

Bang-Bang to PID Cascade

  • Used the line sensor to pick left or right correction, then fed those commands into the wheel speed loop
  • Exponential smoothing reduced in-rush current by about 150 mA, and with a 50 ms control loop the robot completed the course reliably within the one-week build window

Project 03 - Mechanical and Manufacturing

FORKRUNNER

A machined mobile robot with a working forklift mechanism, built mostly from raw stock aluminum and designed around incline climbing, lifting, and transport.

Gear Reduction

344:1 (verified safety margin)

Incline Rating

25 degrees worst-case traversal

Lift

Lead-screw, empirically characterized

Fabrication

Manual mill and lathe from stock aluminum

Outcome

Climbed incline and transported objects

Chassis & Drivetrain

  • Used worst-case torque analysis to size the drivetrain for a 25 degree incline, which led to a 344:1 reduction with a safe margin
  • Designed the chassis around alignment, stiffness, and manufacturability, then machined the parts myself on a manual mill and lathe from stock aluminum

Forklift Mechanism

  • Designed a lead-screw lift because it gave controlled vertical motion and a practical force advantage for lifting objects
  • Sized the lift around payload force and carriage mass, used guided vertical motion to reduce binding, and finished with a forklift that could lift and transport objects
